\section{Summary}

The optimizer keeps a Pareto set of partial plans over two objectives, latency and money, instead of one scalar
cost, and picks one plan at the end (fastest within a budget by default). This report tests it in three settings.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Real Wayang, Java + Apache Spark} (Scenarios A--C): eight workloads, including TPC-H Q1/Q3 on SF1
data, WordCount on 754\,MB, k-means and SGD with loops, and Wayang's synthetic optimizer plans.
\item \textbf{Simulator with a budget} (Scenario D): 19 plan shapes (chains, joins, small and random DAGs up to 25
operators, 4--8 platforms). Five pruning methods are compared on the number of stored partial plans and on the plan
returned to the user.
\item \textbf{Scalability} (Scenario E): chains up to 200 operators, random DAGs up to 35 operators.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Main results.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Same answer as original Wayang when nothing is bounded.} With equal platform rates, all 8 workloads
pick the identical plan (same execution operators). With different rates, our cheapest pick equals original Wayang
(which minimises money) and our fastest pick equals original Wayang run with cost $=$ time, on all 7 workloads.
Executing TPC-H Q3: 7.06\,s original vs.\ 7.14\,s ours, same plan, same 11{,}620 rows.
\item \textbf{Under a budget, original Wayang fails.} It returned an over-budget plan in 19 of 19 graphs (76--89\%
more money than allowed). Exact Pareto and ours returned the true best plan in 19 of 19.
\item \textbf{Pruning.} Our cross-group pruning stores 1.3$\times$ (chains) to 47.9$\times$ (25-operator random DAGs)
fewer partial plans than exact Pareto, with \emph{identical} results. On random DAGs with 20 and 25 operators it stores
0.53$\times$ and 0.22$\times$ as many partial plans as original Wayang (which keeps one plan per group).
\item \textbf{Capped variants} ($k=8$ plans per group) store up to 55$\times$ fewer plans than exact on long chains.
The returned plan is on average 3.0\% (ours-$k8$) or 3.3\% (Pareto-$k8$) slower than the best (worst 9.5\% and
13.4\%), and never over budget.
\item \textbf{Limits.} Exact Pareto runs out of memory (8\,GB) at 30-operator random DAGs; with cross-group pruning
the limit moves to about 35 operators. Cross-group pruning is so far implemented in the simulator only
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}).
\end{itemize}

\section{System under test}

\subsection{What the optimizer does}
Each partial plan carries a cost vector $(c_1, c_2) =$ (latency in ms, money). For an element $e$ (operator,
conversion operator, or loop body) on platform $p(e)$ with time interval $t_e$:
\[
c_1 = \sum_e s(t_e) + \sum_{p\in P}\mathit{init}_p,\qquad
c_2 = \sum_e s\bigl(r_{p(e)}\, t_e\bigr) + \sum_{p\in P}\bigl(\mathit{fix}_p + r_p\,\mathit{init}_p\bigr),
\]
where $s$ is Wayang's interval squasher (geometric mean), $r_p$ = \texttt{wayang.<p>.costs.per-ms},
$\mathit{fix}_p$ = \texttt{costs.fix}, $\mathit{init}_p$ = \texttt{wayang.<p>.init.ms}, and $P$ is the set of
platforms the plan uses. Both axes are sums, so the cost of a combined plan is the sum of its parts. This is what
makes pruning partial plans safe. With equal rates, $c_2$ is exactly Wayang's original scalar cost.

After every concatenation, plans are grouped by (platforms used, operators with still-open connections), as in
Wayang's original pruning. Each group keeps its Pareto front instead of one plan. Optional settings:
\begin{itemize}
\item a cap of $k$ plans per group, which coarsens a group's front when it is larger and always keeps the fastest
and the cheapest plan;
\item budget/deadline pruning: drop plans already over a bound when their group still has a plan within it.
\end{itemize}
At the end: the fastest plan with money $\le$ budget, or the plan with the smallest violation if none fits.

\section{Scenario D: simulator, five pruning methods under a budget}\label{sec:sim}

\subsection{Simulator}
\texttt{PlanDagSimulator} (test code) runs the same dynamic programme as the optimizer on a DAG of operators. It
visits operators in topological order and extends every kept partial plan by every platform. Plans are grouped by
the platforms of the operators that still have an unvisited successor, which is the analogue of Wayang's (platforms,
open operators) grouping. Costs are additive:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Operator cost} on platform $p\in\{0,\dots,P-1\}$: latency $= \mathit{base}_L \cdot (0.45 + 1.3\,p/(P-1))$,
money $= \mathit{base}_M \cdot (1.75 - 1.35\,p/(P-1))$. Platform 0 is the fastest and most expensive (cluster-like);
platform $P-1$ is the slowest and cheapest (single-node-like). With $P=4$ the factors are latency
0.45/0.88/1.32/1.75 and money 1.75/1.30/0.85/0.40.
\item \textbf{Base costs} (latency, money): scan (20, 8), filter (16, 6), map (24, 10), join (80, 22),
aggregate (36, 12), sink (12, 5).
\item \textbf{Conversion} between platforms $a,b$ on every edge: latency $5|a-b|$, money $4|a-b|$ (0 on the same
platform).
\item \textbf{Random DAGs}: a random spanning tree plus 1 to $n/2$ extra forward edges. Each (operator, platform)
latency and money is multiplied independently by a uniform factor in $[0.6, 1.4]$ (fixed seeds 1--3).
\end{itemize}
These platforms are abstract cost profiles, not calibrated Java or Spark. They give every operator a genuine
latency/money trade-off, which real small inputs do not (Scenario A).

\paragraph{Cross-group pruning.} Two partial plans in different groups differ in the platforms of some open
operators. For any completion, $p$'s conversions out of an open operator cost at most $\mathrm{conv}(a\to b)$ less
than $q$'s, where $a, b$ are the two platforms (triangle inequality). So if
$c(q) + \sum_{\text{open edges}} \mathrm{conv}(a_j \to b_j)$ dominates $c(p)$, every completion of $q$ dominates
the same completion of $p$, and $p$ is dropped. The best ``switched-in'' vector per group is computed one open operator
at a time (a separable transform over 2-D fronts), not over all pairs of groups. \texttt{CrossGroupPruningTest}
checks that the final front equals brute force on 25 random DAGs and equals exact Pareto on 18 more graphs.

\paragraph{Budget.} For every graph, budget $=$ cheapest money $+\,0.4\times$(most expensive $-$ cheapest money)
on the true Pareto front, so the fastest plan is never affordable. Budget pruning (as in \texttt{ParetoPruningStrategy}):
within a group, plans above the budget are dropped when the group still has a plan within it.

\paragraph{Graphs.} Two chains (30 operators $\times$ 4 platforms, 60 $\times$ 8); a triple diamond (11 ops), a bushy plan (8),
a branching plan (10); left-deep joins over 8, 16 and 32 tables (scan and join interleaved, as Wayang combines a
join tree; 16, 32 and 64 operators); random DAGs with 12, 16, 20 operators (seeds 1--3) and 25 operators
(seeds 1, 2). Four platforms unless stated.

\paragraph{Counters.} \emph{Generated}: partial plans created before pruning, summed over all steps. \emph{Stored}:
partial plans kept after pruning, summed over all steps (total memory traffic). \emph{Peak}: the most partial plans
alive after one step (peak memory). \emph{Final}: plans reported at the end. \emph{Brute force}: $P^{n}$ complete
plans.

\textbf{Reading the factors.} The gain of cross-group pruning over exact Pareto is not a constant; it grows with

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Cross-group pruning is in the simulator only.} In Wayang, the switching cost between two plans'
open operators has to come from the channel-conversion graph. The triangle inequality holds for the metric that graph
minimises; for the second objective it has to be checked.
\item \textbf{Simulator costs are synthetic.} They show how the methods scale and compare, not real runtimes.
\item \textbf{Platform parameters are Wayang's defaults} (2700\,MHz, Spark start-up 4.5\,s, rates 1 or 128 per ms),
not measured on the test machine. Money is in abstract units. A calibration script
(\texttt{calibrate\_costs.py} in \texttt{benchmark-run}: measured start-up and per-platform time stretch, cloud prices per vCPU)
is ready but was not run for this report.
\item \textbf{Loops.} Wayang adds the loop feedback junction after pruning the loop body. Exact pruning
($\varepsilon=0$, the default) still returned the right plan; $\varepsilon=0.1$ returned a k-means plan 0.05\%
slower than the fastest.
\item \textbf{Execution was measured for one query} (TPC-H Q3, SF1, Spark \texttt{local[1]}).
\end{itemize}
